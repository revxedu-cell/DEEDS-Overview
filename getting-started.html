Map a DEEDS Unit

Start with the end in mind: What will students need to know and be able to do, and how will they use that learning to create meaningful change in the world around them?

Unit Design

Create a strong overview for your DEEDS unit by reflecting on each of the following key considerations. Document your thinking in the Overview section of the DEEDS Unit Planning Template.

Steps
1
Determine Learning Goals Standards & competencies

A strong DEEDS unit is built around clear learning targets — the content standards and competencies students will develop through the work. Identifying these before you design the challenge ensures the project does real academic work, not just real-world work.

Content standards Start with the disciplinary standards your students need to master. Standards are core to DEEDS design, shaping what students investigate in Examine, what they build in Engineer, and what they reflect on in Share. Choose standards substantive enough to require genuine inquiry and application.
Competencies Identify the personal, professional, or civic competencies your school or program prioritizes. DEEDS projects naturally develop skills like communication, collaboration, evidence-based reasoning, and self-direction — but naming the specific competencies you're targeting helps you design for them deliberately.
There's no single entry point In practice, learning goals and challenge design develop together. You might start with standards and find a challenge that requires them, or start with a compelling community issue and map the standards it demands. Both work.
2
Identify a Relevant Challenge Real-world problem

A strong DEEDS unit is anchored in a challenge that exists in the world — real enough to matter, local enough for students to impact, and substantial enough to require the learning teachers are responsible for.

Ground the work in an actual need, tension, problem, or opportunity. The challenge should exist beyond school and matter to people in a real context.
Choose something close enough for students to care about. It should connect to students' lives, communities, futures, or sense of responsibility.
Make sure the challenge requires students to learn what the standards demand. Students should need to build disciplinary knowledge, use important concepts, and apply core skills to respond well.
Choose a challenge that can lead to meaningful action. The work should point toward designing, improving, informing, advocating, building, or testing something real.
Example challenges
–A local food pantry can't keep up with demand — and the community doesn't know it exists.
–An aging community garden is underused, but families nearby don't have access to fresh produce.
–A neighborhood creek floods every spring, damaging homes and green spaces.
There's no single entry point In practice, challenge design moves in both directions. Begin with standards and find the right challenge, or begin with a real community issue and map what students will need to learn. Both work.
3
Connect with Community Partners Voices & expertise

Real partners — those closest to the challenge and those working to solve it — make the work more informed, relevant, and accountable. They also signal to students that what they're doing matters beyond the classroom.

Client experiencing the challenge People living with the problem help students understand what the challenge feels like, what is at stake, and what would actually be useful.
Organization already working on the issue Community groups, nonprofits, agencies, or local institutions can help students understand what is already being tried and where there's room to contribute.
Professional or expert perspective Professionals bring disciplinary knowledge, criteria, tools, and feedback that help students do more credible work.
Example partners
–A shelter director who can speak to what families actually need when they arrive.
–A city parks department managing the green space students are designing around.
–A civil engineer who can review a drainage proposal and give real feedback.
4
Define the Solution Space Design for student agency

In DEEDS, the level of openness in a challenge is intentionally designed. Younger learners work within a bounded set of well-structured solution options. As students get older, the solution space opens up: tradeoffs become less obvious, criteria become more contested, and ultimately students define the problem and generate their own response. This progression is intentional. Structure helps students develop the skills and habits they need. Increasing openness then gives them the space to apply those skills with developing judgment, independence, and creativity.

Design move The solution space should feel slightly beyond what students could do alone — structured enough to support success, open enough to require genuine thinking.
Bounded choice (K–2) Two or three well-structured options. The challenge is real; the solution pathway is defined enough to be manageable and build early confidence.
Guided inquiry (3–8) Multiple open-ended options with real tradeoffs. Students evaluate solutions rather than just choose them, developing judgment about what makes a response strong.
Open inquiry (9–12) Students unpack the challenge, define the problem, and generate their own solution from the ground up — doing the full work of a disciplined problem-solver.
5
Develop the Challenge Question Throughline for the unit

The challenge question isn't just the starting point — it's the throughline. It names the real challenge, keeps the people served visible, and gives the work direction at every phase.

Challenge question frame
How might we address [a real challenge] so that [a specific community] can [experience meaningful improvement]?
Make it real Ground the question in an actual place, partner, need, or condition.
Keep it open Students should need to learn, analyze, and investigate before they can respond well.
Point toward action The question should lead naturally to designing, improving, building, informing, or advocating.
Name who it serves Keep the people most affected visible throughout the learning journey.
Example challenge questions
–How might we help a local food pantry reach more families in our neighborhood?
–How might we redesign a community garden so more families can grow their own food?
–How might we reduce flooding along a local creek and protect the surrounding ecosystem?
Design move Start with a question sturdy enough to launch the work, then refine it as students deepen their understanding of the challenge, the context, and the people affected.
6
Map the Learning Journey Phase-by-phase plan

Use the Learning Journey section of the DEEDS Unit Planning Template to map what students will learn, do, and produce in each phase. Each phase should set up the next: a Discover deliverable specific enough for Examine to investigate, a solution direction from Examine that Engineer can build, a solution from Engineer that Do can put into action, and evidence from Do that Share can present.

Learning Target(s)

One or more "I can... so that..." statements naming the standards or skills students will master, framed within the challenge.

Key Learning Experiences

The activities that build knowledge and connection to the challenge. Some are core DEEDS protocols from the phase pages; others you'll design yourself. Facilitate the investigation and let students do the intellectual work.

Deliverable(s)

What students produce to show their learning. A strong deliverable demonstrates the phase's success criteria and feeds into the final solution, rather than standing alone.

The Learning Journey
Phase Learning Target(s) Key Learning Experiences Deliverable(s)
Discover Full guidance → "I can... so that..." naming what students will know or do by the end of Discover. How will students encounter the challenge firsthand and connect to it emotionally? Simulations, field trips, client interviews, or compelling data. Shows students understand the challenge and its impacts. Where possible, a component of the end product (an intro slide, an early problem definition).
Examine Full guidance → "I can" statements naming the standards and skills students need, contextualized in the challenge. How will students lead their own investigation? Labs, research, text analysis, mini lessons, or station rotations, with formative checkpoints along the way. Demonstrates core content and skills while building toward the Engineer solution: a CER-based argument, a research brief, an annotated diagram.
Engineer Full guidance → "I can" statements naming the design skills and content needed to address a root cause. How will students develop a plan, build with community partner support, and exchange feedback across iterations? The real solution carried into Do: a complete product, a finished proposal, or a working system, built to a standard students are proud to put into action.
Do Full guidance → "I can" statements naming what students need to implement and evaluate their solution. How will students implement their solution, collect data, and reflect on its effectiveness? Evidence of impact students collect and analyze, giving them something concrete to draw on in Share.
Share Full guidance → "I can" statements naming the communication skills students need to present and defend their work. How will students share with a public audience? Decide whether a summative assessment fits your unit. Shows students' growth across the full journey, not just the final phase.
