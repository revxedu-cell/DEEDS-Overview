Map a DEEDS Unit

Start with the end in mind: What will students need to know and be able to do, and how will they use that learning to create meaningful change in the world around them?

Unit Design

Create a strong overview for your DEEDS unit by reflecting on each of the following key considerations. Document your thinking in the Overview section of the DEEDS Unit Planning Template.

Steps

Start by identifying what students need to learn.

Clear learning goals ensure that the unit does meaningful academic work while students address a real challenge.

Content standards

Choose the disciplinary standards students need to master.

These standards shape:

  • What students investigate in Examine
  • What knowledge and skills they apply in Engineer
  • What evidence they analyze in Do
  • What learning they explain and defend in Share

Choose standards that require students to investigate, reason, and apply what they learn. Avoid standards that can be addressed through a quick activity or surface-level project.

Competencies

Identify the personal, professional, or civic competencies students will develop.

DEEDS units often strengthen communication, collaboration, evidence-based reasoning, self-direction, and problem-solving. Name the specific competencies you want students to practice so you can build them into the unit on purpose.

There is no single starting point Learning goals and challenge design often develop together. You may begin with required standards and find a challenge that demands them. You may also begin with a pressing community challenge and identify the standards students must use to address it. Both approaches work.

Anchor the unit in a challenge that exists beyond school.

The challenge should matter to real people, connect to students' lives or communities, and require students to use the content you need to teach. Choose a challenge that:

Meets a real need Ground the work in an actual problem, tension, need, or opportunity. Someone outside the classroom should care about the outcome.
Feels relevant to students Connect the challenge to students' lives, communities, interests, futures, or sense of responsibility.
Requires the learning goals Students should need to build disciplinary knowledge, use key concepts, and apply important skills to respond well. If they can address the challenge without learning the content, choose a different challenge.
Leads to meaningful action The challenge should give students a real opportunity to design, build, improve, test, inform, or advocate for something.
Example challenges
–A local food pantry cannot keep up with demand, and many families do not know its services are available.
–A neighborhood garden is underused while nearby families have limited access to fresh produce.
–A local creek floods each spring, damaging homes and green spaces.

Community partners make the work more informed, useful, and accountable.

They help students understand the challenge, learn from people already working on it, and create solutions that meet real needs. Their involvement also shows students that the work matters beyond the classroom.

A unit may include one partner who fills several roles or different partners at different points in the process.

People directly affected People who experience the challenge can help students understand what is happening, what is at stake, and what support would actually be useful.
Organizations working on the issue Community groups, nonprofits, agencies, businesses, and local institutions can show students what people are already doing and where student work could contribute.
Professionals and experts Professionals can introduce disciplinary tools, methods, criteria, and vocabulary. They can also give feedback that helps students produce more credible work.
Example partners
–A shelter director who can explain what families need when they arrive
–A parks department that manages the green space students are helping redesign
–A civil engineer who can review a drainage proposal and give technical feedback

Decide how much of the solution students will define for themselves.

The solution space should become more open as students gain experience, knowledge, and independence.

K–2 · Bounded choice Give students two or three clear options. The challenge remains real, but you define enough of the pathway to make the work manageable and help students build confidence.
Grades 3–8 · Guided inquiry Give students several possible directions with meaningful tradeoffs. Students compare ideas, apply criteria, and decide which approach offers the strongest response.
Grades 9–12 · Open inquiry Students examine the challenge, define the specific problem they will address, and develop their own solution direction. They take on the full work of a disciplined problem-solver.

You choose the level of openness on purpose. Younger or less experienced students need enough structure to succeed. As students build skills and judgment, they should take more responsibility for defining the problem, evaluating options, and shaping the solution.

Design move Set the work slightly beyond what students could complete alone. Provide enough structure to support success while leaving enough openness to require real thinking and decision-making.

The challenge question gives the whole unit direction.

It names the real challenge, keeps the people affected visible, and helps students connect their learning across every DEEDS phase.

Challenge question frame
How might we address [a real challenge] so that [a specific community] can [experience a meaningful improvement]?

A strong challenge question should:

Make the context clear Name a real place, partner, group, need, or condition.
Require investigation Students should need to learn, analyze evidence, and understand the challenge before they can respond.
Point toward action The question should lead students toward designing, building, improving, testing, informing, or advocating.
Name who the work serves Keep the people most affected by the challenge visible in the question and throughout the unit.
Example challenge questions
–How might we help a local food pantry reach more families in our neighborhood?
–How might we redesign a community garden so more families can grow fresh food?
–How might we reduce flooding along a local creek while protecting the surrounding ecosystem?
Design move Begin with a question that is clear enough to launch the unit. Refine it as students learn more about the challenge, its context, and the people affected.

Use the Learning Journey section of the DEEDS Unit Planning Template to map what students will learn, do, and produce in each phase.

Build a connected journey. Each phase should prepare students for the next:

  • Discover defines the challenge that Examine will investigate.
  • Examine produces a defensible solution direction for Engineer.
  • Engineer creates a solution that students can put into action during Do.
  • Do generates evidence that students can analyze and present during Share.
  • Share brings together learning and evidence from the full unit.
What to Plan for Each Phase

Learning Targets

Write one or more "I can... so that..." statements that name what students will learn or do and why it matters within the challenge.

Key Learning Experiences

Identify the experiences that will help students build knowledge, skills, and connection to the challenge. Some experiences will use core DEEDS protocols; you will design others to fit your content and context. Plan the experience carefully, then let students do the thinking, investigating, discussing, and decision-making.

Deliverables

Identify what students will produce to show their learning and move the work forward. A strong deliverable:

  • Demonstrates the success criteria for the phase
  • Gives you useful evidence of student learning
  • Contributes to the next phase or the final solution
  • Has a clear purpose beyond completing an assignment
