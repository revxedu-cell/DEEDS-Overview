DEEDS — Learning That Matters.

DEEDS is a project-based learning framework that organizes student work around real community challenges, disciplinary inquiry, and meaningful action.

Designing the Unit
Start with something real

A strong DEEDS unit is anchored in a challenge that exists in the world — real enough to matter, local enough for scholars to impact, and substantial enough to require the learning teachers are responsible for.

Ground the work in an actual need, tension, problem, or opportunity. The challenge should exist beyond school and matter to people in a real context.
Choose something close enough for scholars to care about. It should connect to scholars' lives, communities, futures, or sense of responsibility.
Make sure the challenge requires scholars to learn what the standards demand. Scholars should need to build disciplinary knowledge, use important concepts, and apply core skills to respond well.
Choose a challenge that can lead to meaningful action. The work should point toward designing, improving, informing, advocating, building, or testing something real.
Example challenges
–A local food pantry can't keep up with demand — and the community doesn't know it exists.
–An aging community garden is underused, but families nearby don't have access to fresh produce.
–A neighborhood creek floods every spring, damaging homes and green spaces.
There's no single entry point In practice, challenge design moves in both directions. Begin with standards and find the right challenge, or begin with a real community issue and map what scholars will need to learn. Both work.
Community
Bring the community in

Real partners — those closest to the challenge and those working to solve it — make the work more informed, relevant, and accountable. They also signal to scholars that what they're doing matters beyond the classroom.

Client experiencing the challenge People living with the problem help scholars understand what the challenge feels like, what is at stake, and what would actually be useful.
Organization already working on the issue Community groups, nonprofits, agencies, or local institutions can help scholars understand what is already being tried and where there's room to contribute.
Professional or expert perspective Professionals bring disciplinary knowledge, criteria, tools, and feedback that help scholars do more credible work.
Example partners
–A shelter director who can speak to what families actually need when they arrive.
–A city parks department managing the green space scholars are designing around.
–A civil engineer who can review a drainage proposal and give real feedback.
Challenge Question
Frame the work as a question

The challenge question isn't just the starting point — it's the throughline. It names the real challenge, keeps the people served visible, and gives the work direction at every phase.

Challenge question frame
How might we address [a real challenge] so that [a specific community] can [experience meaningful improvement]?
Make it real Ground the question in an actual place, partner, need, or condition.
Keep it open Scholars should need to learn, analyze, and investigate before they can respond well.
Point toward action The question should lead naturally to designing, improving, building, informing, or advocating.
Name who it serves Keep the people most affected visible throughout the learning journey.
Example challenge questions
–How might we help a local food pantry reach more families in our neighborhood?
–How might we redesign a community garden so more families can grow their own food?
–How might we reduce flooding along a local creek and protect the surrounding ecosystem?
Design move Start with a question sturdy enough to launch the work, then refine it as scholars deepen their understanding of the challenge, the context, and the people affected.
Solution Design
Match the solution space to your learners

In DEEDS, the level of openness in a challenge is intentionally designed. Younger learners work within a bounded set of well-structured solution options. As students get older, the solution space opens up: tradeoffs become less obvious, criteria become more contested, and ultimately students define the problem and generate their own response. This progression is intentional. Structure helps students develop the skills and habits they need. Increasing openness then gives them the space to apply those skills with developing judgment, independence, and creativity.

Bounded choice (K–2) Two or three well-structured options. The challenge is real; the solution pathway is defined enough to be manageable and build early confidence.
Guided inquiry (3–8) Multiple open-ended options with real tradeoffs. Students evaluate solutions rather than just choose them, developing judgment about what makes a response strong.
Open inquiry (9–12) Students unpack the challenge, define the problem, and generate their own solution from the ground up — doing the full work of a disciplined problem-solver.
Design move The solution space should feel slightly beyond what students could do alone — structured enough to support success, open enough to require genuine thinking.

Explore the Framework

The Five Phases

Phase 01 Discover Scholars see, hear, and feel the challenge before they study it — building empathy and a personal "why." Phase 02 Examine Scholars investigate the challenge deeply using disciplinary content, skills, and evidence. Phase 03 Engineer Scholars design, prototype, and refine a response to the challenge. Phase 04 Do Scholars implement their solution and take real action in the community. Phase 05 Share Scholars present their work publicly and reflect on what they've learned and contributed.
