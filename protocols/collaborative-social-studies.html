Phase 1
Close Reading & Annotation
10 min, independent
Read the text closely, once, on your own.
Identify the central idea or argument and annotate the evidence that supports it.
Mark key terms and anything unclear.
C3: Gathering, Evaluating, and Using Evidence
Phase 2
Analysis Using a Disciplinary Lens
10 min, independent or pairs
Re-read with a focus question, using a lens (cause/effect, comparison, or economic reasoning).
Study a second source (map, graph, timeline, or dataset) and connect it to the text.
Explain your thinking using evidence from both sources.
C3: Chronological Reasoning and Causation, Comparison and Contextualization, Geographic Reasoning, Economics
Phase 3
Written Argument
10 min, independent
Make a claim about the issue the text raises.
Support your claim with evidence from the text and the second source.
Address at least one counterargument or limitation.
C3: Gathering, Evaluating, and Using Evidence, Communicating and Critiquing Conclusions
Phase 4
Civic Discussion & Informed Action
10 min, small group then whole class
Discuss your claim in a small group and respond to others' reasoning.
As a class, identify where you agree and disagree, and why.
Consider what action, if any, this issue calls for.
C3: Civic Participation, Taking Informed Action

Why this, why now
This routine builds social studies habits of mind: gathering and weighing evidence, reasoning about cause and effect or comparison, and taking informed civic action. Younger students build these habits through a shared text and repeated reading; older students build them through independent close reading and a defensible written argument. Reading skill develops alongside that thinking, not instead of it.

Hattie
Classroom Discussion
Both bands end in discussion: sharing an idea at K-5, defending a claim and taking informed action at 6-12. Structured discussion ranks among the highest-impact practices in Hattie's research.
Facilitation Notes: K–5
  • The repeated reading exists to make the who/what of the text accessible before you ask students to reason about it, not just to build fluency. Don't cut it under time pressure.
  • Keep Phase 4's new source concrete: a labeled map or a simple timeline works better than a data table at this age.
  • Phase 5 should stay close to home. "Our class" or "our neighborhood" is the right scale for civic participation at this age, not a distant policy question.
Facilitation Notes: 6–12
  • Independent close reading is what lets students build their own argument, not a shared one. Resist the urge to add repeated choral reading back in.
  • Phase 2's lens (cause/effect, comparison, or economic reasoning) should be chosen deliberately, based on what the text and the unit call for. Name the lens explicitly before students re-read.
  • Phase 2 depends on having a second source ready. Without one to analyze, this phase collapses into more discussion of the original text.
  • Phase 4's "Taking Informed Action" step is easy to rush. Give it the time it needs. A civic discussion that stops at "we disagree" hasn't done what this phase is for.
Text Selection & Length
  • K–2: up to 150 words   3–8: up to 250 words   9–12: up to 300 words
  • Choose a text with a clear, defensible central idea and enough context for students to reason about cause, comparison, or decisions in Phase 3.
  • Pair the text with a genuinely related second source (map, graph, timeline, image, or dataset) for Phase 4. The two sources should inform each other, not repeat the same information.
